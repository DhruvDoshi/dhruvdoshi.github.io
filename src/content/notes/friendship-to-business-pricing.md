I did 360 hours of architect-level work for CAD 11,500. That is CAD 32 an hour — intern rates — for designing and building an entire multi-tenant SaaS platform: four POS integrations, a reward engine, a 22-page merchant dashboard, a campaign system, full infrastructure. A consultancy would have quoted CAD 80,000 to 120,000 for the same scope. I charged less than a tenth of that, because it was a friend.

This note is about the framework shift I had to make: from "friend doing a favor" to professional engagement. Not bitter. Honest. If you have ever undercharged because of a relationship, this is for you.

## Why the friend discount is so expensive

The friend discount does not just cost you money. It costs you in three ways that are harder to see:

**Scope has no edges.** In a professional engagement, the statement of work defines what is included, and everything else is a change request. In a friend engagement, there is no statement of work — there is a conversation, and then more conversations, and the scope grows with every call. I built white-label branding, a promotions engine, analytics dashboards, and three extra POS integrations "at no extra cost" because the relationship made it awkward to say no. Each one was reasonable on its own. Together they doubled the project.

**You do work beneath your level.** I am an enterprise architect. My profession is designing systems. On this project I also did development, DevOps, QA, and project management — five roles — including grunt work at 2 AM that I would never accept from a client at my actual rate. The friendship made it feel noble. It was not noble; it was unpaid labor subsidized by a relationship.

**Payment becomes a favor instead of an obligation.** When the price is already a gift, asking for the remaining balance feels like asking for a second gift. The CAD 8,500 outstanding was the remainder of an agreed price for delivered work — not a negotiation. But eight months of "don't worry about it" had turned a commercial debt into something socially awkward to collect.

## The shift: what changed in practice

The shift was not emotional. I did not have a fight. I made a structural decision: the friends-framework was over, and from that point forward, everything was professional. Concretely, that meant:

**Written payment terms before more work.** The outstanding balance gets a written payment plan with dates and amounts — not a verbal "don't worry about it". No new scope is discussed until the old money is on paper. This is the single most important rule: never negotiate future work while past work is unpaid, because the two get bundled and the debt gets diluted.

**Separate the buckets.** Past debt, future development, future advisory — three separate conversations, three separate agreements. The development work goes through my company with a proper B2B contract. My own time is architecture and advisory only, at CAD 50 an hour, with a written estimate agreed before any work starts. I am done writing code for free.

**Name the market rate out loud.** I put the numbers on the table: 360 hours, five roles, consultancy quote of 80-120K, paid 11.5K. Not as an accusation — as context. Most undercharging persists because the gap is never stated plainly.

**"Money in, product out."** That became the operating principle. New feature requests — like a custom connector for a closed-source POS with no codebase access — get scoped, priced, and paid before work begins. Willingness to build was never the constraint; willingness to pay is the only variable. Saying this out loud, calmly, is not hostile. It is what doing business looks like.

## What I would do differently

If I could redo the engagement from the start:

1. **Write the statement of work even for friends.** Especially for friends. The document protects the relationship — it is much easier to point at a scope clause than to have an awkward conversation eight months in.
2. **Price the friendship as a defined discount, not an open tab.** "My rate is X, I am giving you 40% off because we are friends" is completely different from "I will figure it out, do not worry about it". The first has a floor. The second has no floor.
3. **Invoice on schedule regardless of relationship.** The moment invoicing becomes awkward, the relationship is already costing you money. Professional rhythm — invoice, payment terms, follow-up — is not unfriendly. It is what lets the friendship survive the business.
4. **Decide your walk-away line in advance.** Mine became: no written payment plan, no further work. Having the line decided before the meeting meant I did not have to find courage in the room — I just had to execute a decision I had already made.

## The uncomfortable truth

Undercharging for friends feels generous, but it is often conflict avoidance wearing generosity's clothes. Real generosity is a defined gift: "I am giving you this much, clearly, on purpose." An undefined discount is an unspecified liability that grows until someone gets hurt.

I believe in the product I built. That has not changed. What changed is the framework: I will not work for free, even for people I like, even for equity, even for the future. Money in, product out. Everything in writing. That is not cynicism. That is the structure that lets you keep building things for people without resenting them.
